# Chapter 2: Digitization of Information

## The End of Continuity

Is nature's "default setting" continuous or discrete? In Chapter 1, we already encountered the material version of this question: can matter be divided indefinitely? The answer is no — the existence of atoms proved the discreteness of matter. But there is a deeper version of this question that remains unanswered: can energy be divided indefinitely? Can an oscillating system increase or decrease its energy in arbitrarily small increments?

Classical physics answered this question with an unequivocal "yes." Within the classical framework composed of Newtonian mechanics and Maxwell's electromagnetic theory, energy is a continuous variable that can take any real value from zero to infinity. A vibrating string can oscillate with an arbitrarily small amplitude, a beam of light can carry an arbitrarily small amount of energy, and an electron's orbit can transition smoothly between any two radii. Continuity is not merely a feature of classical physics — it is practically its soul. The entire edifice of calculus is built upon the assumption of continuity, and calculus is precisely the mathematical language of classical physics. If energy were not continuous, those infinitesimals, those limiting processes, those smooth curves and elegant equations of calculus would all lose their foundation.

Yet the previous chapter concluded with a fundamental difficulty: the continuous framework of classical physics cannot sustain a stable atom. An electron orbiting the nucleus in a Coulomb field must radiate energy according to classical electrodynamics, spiraling into the nucleus on a picosecond timescale. The discrete lines displayed in atomic spectra are utterly incompatible with the continuous radiation predicted by classical theory. The direction for resolving these crises pointed toward a radical solution: discretization. Energy would no longer be a continuous river, but a staircase of distinct steps. The electron would no longer glide smoothly along continuous orbits, but could only reside on specific "steps," with no legitimate intermediate states between them.

This solution was initially introduced into physics as a stopgap measure. In 1900, Planck hypothesized that an oscillator's energy could only take integer multiples of $\varepsilon = h\nu$ in order to explain the blackbody radiation spectrum, and he himself was unsure whether this was a mathematical trick or a law of physics. Five years later, Einstein pushed this idea a giant step further, proposing that light itself is composed of indivisible quanta, each carrying energy $h\nu$. But during the first decade or so, these ideas remained more like scattered sparks of inspiration than a systematic theory of atomic structure. The person who first successfully applied the idea of quantization to the internal structure of the atom was the young Danish physicist Bohr, and the most crucial clue his theory relied upon came not from any theoretical derivation, but from meticulously measured tables of spectral data.

## Silicon Perspective: From Analog to Digital

"Continuous becoming discrete" — this profound transformation in physics has a counterpart in the history of electronic engineering that is surprisingly precise in its parallels.

During the first half of the 20th century, electronic communication technology was built entirely on the foundation of analog signals. Sound was converted into continuously varying voltage waveforms, transmitted through copper wires, and converted back into sound at the receiving end. The amount of information carried by an analog signal is, in principle, infinite — voltage can take any real value, and every minute detail of the waveform carries information. However, analog systems have a fatal weakness: noise. Noise is inevitably superimposed on the signal during transmission, and once it mixes in, it can never be completely removed. After multiple rounds of amplification and transmission, the original signal becomes buried under noise and is distorted beyond recognition. An analog vinyl record degrades slightly with each play, an analog telephone call deteriorates with each relay station — the continuous world is fragile.

In 1948, Claude Shannon published his foundational work on information theory, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," fundamentally changing our understanding of signals. Shannon showed that if a continuous analog signal is discretized — sampled at fixed intervals in time and quantized into a finite number of amplitude levels — the resulting digital signal, though sacrificing some precision, gains absolute immunity to noise. A digital signal has only two states, 0 and 1; as long as noise is not large enough to flip a 0 into a 1, the signal can be perfectly recovered. Digital signals can be copied, transmitted, and stored without any loss, unaffected by distance or time. A CD can be played ten thousand times without losing a single bit of information; a digital telephone call can travel thousands of kilometers through optical fiber without any distortion. Discretization is not a loss of information — it is the salvation of information.

Bohr's "digital renovation" of the atom and Shannon's digital renovation of communication are isomorphic at a deep structural level. The classical atom is like an analog system: the electron can move in any orbit (a continuous state space), can radiate electromagnetic waves of any frequency (continuous output), but this system is unstable — the electron continuously radiates energy and collapses, just as an analog signal continuously degrades during transmission. Bohr's quantization condition transformed this fragile analog system into a robust digital one: the electron can only occupy a finite number of discrete energy levels (a quantized state space), can only emit a finite set of frequencies of light (discrete output), and does not radiate at all when in the lowest energy level (the absolute stability of the ground state). Just as the stability of digital signals comes from discretization eliminating the accumulation of noise, the stability of atoms comes from the discretization of energy levels eliminating continuous energy leakage. Quantization is not a "defect" of nature; quite the opposite, it is the mechanism nature uses to protect itself from collapse — discreteness endows atoms with permanence.

But this solution did not arise from thin air. Long before Rutherford's scattering experiment, physicists had already accumulated a vast amount of data on atomic emission. The light emitted by each element in a discharge tube, when dispersed through a prism, reveals a set of discrete, precisely positioned bright lines — the atomic spectrum. These spectral data are like encrypted signals transmitted from within the atom, carrying key information about its internal structure and waiting to be decoded. Spectroscopists spent decades meticulously measuring the wavelengths of these spectral lines, accumulating vast tables of data, yet no one could discern the pattern behind these numbers. It was not until Balmer and Rydberg, guided by pure mathematical intuition, distilled astonishingly concise empirical formulas from these data that the way was paved for Bohr's later theoretical breakthrough. In a sense, spectral data were the earliest "digital signals" from nature ever recorded by humankind — they were discrete from the very beginning; the physicists of that era simply did not yet realize that "discrete" was itself the answer.

This chapter tells the story of that decoding journey. First, starting from the problem of blackbody radiation, we will see how Kirchhoff used the second law of thermodynamics to prove a universal law of thermal radiation, and how this problem evolved over forty years into the ultraviolet catastrophe — a complete rout of classical physics that ultimately forced Planck in 1900 to introduce the hypothesis of energy quantization, raising the curtain on quantum theory. Next, we will see how Balmer and Rydberg distilled astonishingly concise empirical formulas from the ocean of spectral data — a data-mining feat worthy of being called "human-powered symbolic regression." The Rydberg formula can precisely reconstruct all spectral lines of the hydrogen atom using just one constant and two integers; this remarkable data compression efficiency strongly hints at the existence of deep physical laws behind the data. Then, we will discuss in detail how Bohr "pixelated" the electron's continuous orbits into discrete energy levels by introducing an angular momentum quantization condition, thereby deriving the Rydberg formula from theory and giving physical meaning to the Rydberg constant. Bohr's theory was the first to express an empirical constant in terms of fundamental physical constants ($m_e$, $e$, $\hbar$, $c$), achieving the leap from "description" to "understanding." Finally, the Franck-Hertz experiment will demonstrate the discreteness of atomic energy levels in a manner entirely different from spectroscopy, using electron collisions to provide the most direct "hardware-level" verification of the quantization hypothesis. The ingenuity of the Franck-Hertz experiment lies in bypassing optical measurements altogether, employing an almost "brute-force" approach — gradually increasing the kinetic energy of electrons and observing when atoms suddenly "accept" energy — to directly probe the staircase structure of atomic energy levels.

From a methodological perspective, this chapter showcases multiple complementary modes of cognition in physics. Blackbody radiation and Planck's formula illustrate the classic path from experimental anomaly to theoretical breakthrough — when classical theory yields an absurd prediction (the ultraviolet catastrophe), physicists are compelled to introduce entirely new assumptions. Spectroscopy and the Balmer formula represent the "bottom-up" approach of empirical induction — extracting patterns from data without explaining their origin. The Bohr model represents the "top-down" approach of theoretical deduction — deriving testable predictions from postulates. The Franck-Hertz experiment represents independent verification — testing the same theoretical prediction through an entirely different experimental method. The synergistic interplay of these modes is the methodological cornerstone upon which modern physics steadily advances.
