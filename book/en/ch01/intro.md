# Chapter 1 The Nuclear Structure of the Atom

## Can Matter Be Divided Forever?

Pick up a stone and break it in half. Take one of those halves and break it again. Keep going, keep splitting — can this process continue forever? What would you ultimately end up with?

This seemingly naive question has occupied some of humanity's finest minds for twenty-five centuries. In the fifth century BCE, the ancient Greek philosopher Democritus gave a resolute answer: no. He believed that once you divide matter down to a certain scale, you arrive at an indivisible smallest particle, which he called atomos — Greek for "uncuttable." In Democritus's view, the entire universe is nothing more than these indivisible particles moving, colliding, and combining in the void. Every macroscopic phenomenon — the flow of water, the burning of fire, the birth and death of living things — is ultimately just a rearrangement of atoms.

But his contemporary, the towering figure of Aristotle, disagreed entirely. He held that matter is continuous and can be divided without limit; you never reach a "smallest unit." Space is full; there is no void. In Aristotle's view, Democritus's atomism was logically flawed: if an atom is an entity with a definite size, then it has a left half and a right half, and should therefore be further divisible; if an atom has no size at all, then how can infinitely many sizeless things combine to form an object with finite extent?

The essence of this debate is a profound philosophical question: **Is the fundamental constitution of nature discrete or continuous?** In modern mathematical language, is the structure of matter more like the integers or more like the real numbers? Integers are separated one by one, with gaps between them; the real numbers form an unbroken continuum, with infinitely many numbers between any two. If matter is "integer-like," then a smallest unit exists; if matter is "real-number-like," then division can go on forever.

The remarkable thing is that this question cannot be settled within a purely philosophical framework. You cannot determine through logical reasoning alone whether matter is discrete or continuous — both possibilities are logically self-consistent. Aristotle's arguments are as elegant as those of Democritus; the match ended in a draw. This stalemate persisted for over two millennia, until humanity invented a new method of adjudication: experiment.

In humanity's long journey toward understanding nature, the concept of the "atom" underwent precisely this transformation from philosophical speculation to scientific evidence. In the early 19th century, Dalton proposed his atomic theory, using the concept of atoms to explain the law of definite proportions and the law of multiple proportions in chemical reactions, thereby transforming the atom from a philosophical concept into a scientific hypothesis. Chemical experiments provided the first body of indirect evidence: substances always combine in fixed ratios during chemical reactions, strongly suggesting that matter is composed of discrete fundamental units. Yet what did an atom actually look like? Was it truly indivisible? These questions remained without definitive answers until the end of the 19th century.

The late 19th century was a remarkable period in the history of physics. On one hand, the edifice of classical physics had been largely completed: Newtonian mechanics, Maxwell's electromagnetic theory, thermodynamics, and statistical mechanics formed what appeared to be a complete theoretical framework. On the other hand, a series of new experimental discoveries were shaking the very foundations of this edifice. In 1895, Roentgen discovered X-rays; in 1896, Becquerel discovered radioactivity; in 1897, Thomson discovered the electron. These discoveries revealed that the atom was not indivisible after all and possessed a rich internal structure. Exploring this internal structure became the first major task of 20th-century physics.

This chapter traces how humanity gradually unveiled the mystery of atomic structure. We begin with the discovery of the electron, the first step toward understanding atomic structure. We then describe how the charge and mass of the electron were measured and how Avogadro's number was determined; the precise determination of these fundamental constants laid the quantitative foundation for atomic physics. Next, we discuss in detail the Rutherford scattering experiment, which completely transformed our understanding of atomic structure and established the nuclear model of the atom. We also derive the Rutherford scattering formula, discuss experimental verification, and estimate the size of the atomic nucleus. Finally, we analyze the fundamental difficulties faced by the planetary model within the framework of classical physics, difficulties that ultimately led to the birth of quantum mechanics.

From a methodological perspective, this chapter demonstrates how physicists designed ingenious experiments to "probe" the invisible microscopic world. Atoms are too small to observe directly; we can only infer their structure through indirect means. This method of indirect inference permeates the entire development of atomic physics. In the language of modern computer science, this process can be likened to "reverse engineering": we feed a known signal into a system, observe the system's output response, and then deduce the internal structure of the system from the input-output relationship. Rutherford bombarded atoms with alpha particles and recorded the distribution of scattering angles, effectively reverse-engineering the "black box" of the atom. This chapter presents these topics from both the perspective of physics (Carbon Story) and computer science (Silicon Perspective), helping readers build a multidimensional understanding.
